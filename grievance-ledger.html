The Public Grievance Ledger

A public, aggregated, trustworthy record of what citizens are actually asking for: a demand-side ledger for governance in India. Open source, free, non-commercial, permanently.

The problem

Entry 01 / Why this exists

India's official grievance portals treat complaints as private tickets. There is no public record of what people are collectively asking for, no visibility into whether "resolved" meant anything, and real risk for anyone who complains loudly.

  • Opacity. CPGRAMS and state portals offer no public view of the scale or shape of collective problems.
  • Fake closure. "Disposed" is not "resolved": the government's own feedback calls find only 51–63% satisfaction among complainants whose cases were officially closed.
  • Manipulation. Social media, the de-facto alternative, is astroturfable and unreliable as a measure of genuine demand.
  • Retaliation. Documented and severe: FIRs over a pothole video, 100+ RTI activists killed. The 2014 whistleblower-protection Act was never brought into force.

The bet

Entry 02 / Theory of change

The platform's impact rests on three pillars, deliberately designed to work even if government ignores it entirely:

  • A public ledger: a persistent record making collective problems visible and measurable. Complaining is the write; scorecards, audits and election-time reads all run on the same data.
  • Named accountability: response rates and resolution times by department and jurisdiction, never raw volume rankings.
  • Permissionless routing: every grievance auto-files into the official channel that should own it, no partnership required. FixMyStreet proved the move: 98% of UK councils accepted reports they never agreed to receive.

Deliberate non-goals: not a government CRM, not a social network, not a polling product, and no monetization design, ever. The platform publishes no analyses of its own; journalists, activists and citizens build their reports on top of it.

The first principle

Entry 03 / The constraint that shapes everything
"Complainants should never get identified, ever; that is the soul of this open-source project." Founding principle D5. It admits no exception, including the platform itself

The required form is architectural: the platform cannot identify complainants even if compelled or compromised, because data never held cannot be produced. The recommended identity architecture: prove a valid credential on-device, run it through a threshold OPRF across jurisdictionally-diverse operators, and receive a blindly-issued BBS credential. Per issue, a scoped proof yields an unlinkable pseudonym: ~200 ms, ~1 KB, works offline. Research along the way surfaced that every existing Aadhaar-ZK nullifier design is computable by anyone holding a photocopy, which makes the threshold OPRF a correctness requirement, not an optimisation.

The calls that mattered

Entry 04 / Decision ledger, abridged
RefDecisionWhy it was hard
D9 Two products. V0 collects and routes complaints; V1, the ledger itself, is built only later. Cold start is the period of maximum astroturf leverage, and the leverage came from publishing magnitude. V0 publishes nothing derived, dissolving the launch risk.
D10 A public threshold: 50,000 complaints, with a visible counter. V1 work starts the moment it is crossed. The risk of a two-product plan is that the second product never ships. The public threshold is the commitment device against exactly that.
P4 No astroturf classifier. Describe arrival shapes, make declared collective reports first-class, and anchor magnitude to modelled expected incidence. Research showed distinguishing legitimate collective action from astroturfing is behaviourally undecidable: fifty organised neighbours and fifty party workers produce identical data. So change the estimand until the attack loses its point.
D13 No SMS, no phone number, no PII at V0. Reporter identity is a device-bound WebCrypto key. Storing phone numbers against complaints would make V0 a retaliation database (the exact artefact the architecture exists to prevent), and OTP flows lose 12–40% of the funnel.
· No SLA clock anywhere in the interface. Deadlines surface only as vested statutory entitlements: "the law says you are owed ₹340." Evidence says the demobilising ingredient is unsurprisingness, not negativity. A countdown that expires quietly teaches learned helplessness; a vested entitlement accumulates.
D7 Infrastructure, not a publication. The platform never publishes its own analyses; election windows freeze data rather than hide it. Credibility is the core asset. The moment the platform editorialises, it becomes a participant in the fights it exists to measure.

What the research disallowed

Entry 05 / Constraints found, not chosen

22 sourced research reports set the boundaries. The ones that reshaped the product:

  • No national ward-boundary dataset exists in India. Ward-level publication needs per-city sourcing, so national scope holds only for issue-scale grievances.
  • The digital-access denominator stops at district level. Imputing ward access from district means makes a rich ward and a slum ward identical, so district is the honest floor for headline metrics.
  • Reporting bias is non-monotonic: middle-deprivation areas report most while the most deprived have the most incidents. Per-capita normalisation does not fix it; every count ships with a coverage indicator.
  • The 3 GB RAM cliff. ZK proving fails systematically below it, forcing the lighter BBS path for everyday actions and testing on a ₹15,000 handset from day one.

Where it stands

Entry 06 / Running balance

Discovery closed on 31 Jul 2026 with all four founding design sessions complete and the V0 launch spec drafted, the last document before code:

  • Launch wedge verified: national service-delivery failure (driving-licence and transport services first; a live national counter of 32.09 Cr applications provides the denominator), with Bengaluru as the first hyperlocal city, chosen for Karnataka's Sakala service-guarantee data.
  • Routing table drafted against a verified 92-ministry directory of grievance officers; user-mediated send keeps the platform's copy pseudonymous.
  • Deliverability priced and de-risked at ~₹330/month, including the structural finding that nearly all central-government mail flows through a single gateway, which turned the bounce policy into a launch requirement.
  • 24-month budget costed (₹18–30 lakh for a credible shot at the median timeline) with pre-committed checkpoint bands and kill/pivot triggers, so a correctly-performing month six is not misread as failure.

Next: legal entity formation, deliberately sequenced first because entity registration, sender-domain warm-up and statutory clocks all run on external time that founder effort cannot compress.
